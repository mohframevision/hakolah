## Why Write About This?

News of breaches attributed to AI tools keeps growing, and so do fake messages and scam calls that impersonate a bank, a company or a relative. The best thing an ordinary person can do isn't to panic, but to **reduce the places your data can leak from** and turn on strong protection for your important accounts.

This article walks through it step by step, then says plainly what the Hakolah site collects about you and what it doesn't.

## Rule 1: The Fewer Places Hold Your Data, the Lower the Risk

Every site or app you give your name, email, phone number and birth date to is another door your data can be stolen through, even if you're careful. If you have thirty old accounts you never use, you're carrying thirty weak points for nothing.

**Do:**
- Review your old accounts and delete the ones you don't use, especially apps that asked for a lot of information.
- Before creating a new account, ask: do I really need this service?
- Give each site **the minimum** it asks for, and don't enter your real birth date where it isn't needed.

**Don't:**
- Don't sign up for every new app just because it's free.
- Don't leave old apps connected to your email and card without using them.

## Rule 2: A Different Password for Every Site, and a Manager to Remember Them

Reusing the same password across sites is the riskiest habit: when one small site is breached, the attacker tries the same password on your email and bank account.

**Do:**
- Use a **password manager** that generates a strong password for each site and stores it for you. Free options you'll find on Hakolah: [Bitwarden, KeePassXC and Proton Pass](/en/links-tools/security.html).
- Make your main email password **unique and very long**, since it's the recovery key for your other accounts.

**Don't:**
- Don't keep passwords in a phone note or on paper next to the screen.
- Don't use your name, birth date or "123456".

## Rule 3: Turn On Two-Step Verification, the Right Way

Two-step verification means a password alone isn't enough to get into your account. But not every method is equal:

| Method | Strength | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Physical security key | Strongest | Plugged in or tapped when you sign in |
| Authenticator app (like Google Authenticator) | Strong | Generates a code that changes every half minute |
| SMS text | **Weakest** | Can be intercepted, or the phone company can be tricked into moving your number |

**Do:** turn on app- or key-based verification on your important accounts first: **email, bank, Google or Apple account, and social accounts.**

**Don't:** don't rely on text messages alone if a stronger option is available, **and never give a verification code to anyone**, even if they say they're from the bank or tech support. Real banks never ask you for the code.

## Rule 4: Beware of Scams That Exploit Trust

A scammer doesn't always need a technical break-in; often they just fool you. Common tricks:
- A message or call claiming to be from your bank, a delivery company or an official body, asking for a code or a link.
- A message from a "relative" asking for an urgent transfer after their account was hacked or their identity was copied.
- A call in a familiar voice. Voice cloning with AI is now possible.

**Do:**
- Agree with your family on a **secret word or phrase** that either side says to confirm identity before any money or sensitive-information request, especially with older relatives.
- If someone calls in the name of an organization, **hang up and call the official number yourself**, from its website or your card.
- Slow down: scams depend on urgency.

**Don't:**
- Don't click links from unexpected messages.
- Don't transfer money based on a message alone, even if it seems to come from someone you know.

## Rule 5: Update, and Keep a Backup

Many breaches exploit known flaws that already have a fix, but the device owner didn't update.

**Do:**
- Turn on automatic updates for your device's system, browser and important apps.
- Keep a backup of your important files in a separate place (an external drive or a well-protected cloud) so they aren't lost to ransomware.

**Don't:**
- Don't postpone updates for months. Some programs don't update themselves, like WinRAR, so check its version yourself.
- Don't download cracked software or use untrusted download sites.

## Rule 6: Check Whether Your Data Has Leaked

The well-known site **Have I Been Pwned** lets you enter your email to see if it appeared in past leaks. If it did, change the password on that site and on any site where you used the same one.

## Quick Checklist: Start Today

1. Turn on two-step verification **with an app or key** on your email, bank and Google or Apple account.
2. Give your main email a unique, long password.
3. Install a password manager and move your accounts into it gradually.
4. Delete five old accounts you don't use.
5. Agree with your family on a secret phrase for verification.
6. Turn on automatic updates, and back up your important files.
7. Check your email on Have I Been Pwned.

And if you doubt any message or call, stop and verify from another source before doing anything.
